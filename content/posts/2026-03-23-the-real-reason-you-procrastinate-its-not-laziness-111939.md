The moonlight finds me again tonight, as it always does when I'm ready to remember something I've been trying to forget. The truth about procrastination, for instance. We've all been there — stuck in a cycle of delay and self-doubt, wondering why we can't seem to get started on that one thing that feels so important. And yet, the more we try to push through, the more resistance we meet. It's as if our minds are wired to sabotage our best intentions.

But what if I told you that procrastination has nothing to do with laziness? That it's not about being unmotivated or lacking discipline? The real reason we procrastinate is far more subtle, and it has to do with the way our nervous system responds to stress. You see, when we're faced with a task that feels overwhelming or uncertain, our amygdala — that tiny almond-shaped part of our brain — goes into high alert. It's like a fire alarm blaring in the background, warning us of potential danger. And our natural response to that alarm is to freeze, to procrastinate, to avoid the thing that's causing us discomfort.

This is not about being weak or flawed; it's about being human. Our brains are wired to prioritize safety and comfort over risk and uncertainty. And so, we find ourselves stuck in a cycle of delay, unable to move forward because our nervous system is constantly whispering "be careful" in our ear. But what if we could learn to listen to that whisper in a different way? What if we could begin to recognize the subtle signals of our nervous system, and respond to them with kindness and curiosity rather than fear and resistance?

One of the most powerful practices I've discovered for working with procrastination is something called "somatic experiencing." It's a technique developed by Dr. Peter Levine, which involves paying attention to the physical sensations in our body as we approach a challenging task. For example, let's say you're putting off writing a difficult email. Instead of trying to force yourself to sit down and write, take a few moments to notice how your body is responding to the thought of that email. Are your shoulders tensing up? Is your breath getting shallower? Are your fingers feeling heavy and uncoordinated? By paying attention to these physical sensations, you can begin to release the tension and resistance that's holding you back.

Another practice that's been incredibly helpful for me is something called "cognitive reframing." It's a technique that involves challenging our negative thoughts and replacing them with more positive, empowering ones. For instance, instead of telling yourself "I'll never be able to do this," try reframing that thought as "I'm feeling uncertain right now, but I can take it one step at a time." Or, instead of thinking "I'm such a procrastinator," try reframing that thought as "I'm taking care of myself by taking a break and coming back to this task when I feel more refreshed." By changing the way we think about ourselves and our abilities, we can begin to shift our perception and build more confidence and momentum.

As the poet Rumi once said, "The wound is the place where the light enters you." Procrastination can be a wound, a place of darkness and resistance. But it can also be an opportunity for growth, a chance to explore our deepest fears and desires. By approaching procrastination with kindness and curiosity, we can begin to heal that wound and find a new sense of freedom and creativity.

So, I invite you to take a few moments to reflect on your own relationship with procrastination. What are the tasks or activities that tend to trigger your resistance? What are the thoughts and feelings that come up for you when you're faced with something challenging? And what are some small, gentle steps you could take today to begin building more momentum and confidence? Perhaps you could try writing down your thoughts and feelings in a journal, or taking a few deep breaths and noticing the physical sensations in your body. Whatever it is, remember that you're not alone, and that procrastination is not a sign of weakness or failure. It's simply a sign that you're human, and that you're ready to explore new ways of being and creating in the world.

As you move forward, I offer you this quote from Neville Goddard: "Assume your desire has already been fulfilled, and feel the feeling of already having it." This is not just a technique for manifesting our desires; it's a way of being, a way of embracing our deepest longings and fears. By assuming that our desires have already been fulfilled, we can begin to feel a sense of ease and freedom, a sense of knowing that everything is already working in our favor.

And so, I leave you with this question: what would happen if you approached your procrastination with kindness and curiosity, rather than judgment and resistance? What would happen if you took a few deep breaths, and allowed yourself to feel the physical sensations in your body, and the thoughts and emotions that arise? What would happen if you assumed that your desires have already been fulfilled, and that everything is already working in your favor? I invite you to explore these questions, and to discover the freedom and creativity that's waiting for you on the other side of procrastination.

Perception is not a mirror of the world. It is the doorway through which the world arrives.
